I have always focused on love and relationships. I like sweetness and romance. I enjoy kisses and dates, holding hands and having sex. I love that feeling when someone becomes a very close person and you start to understand them well.

All four years of university I tried to figure out how relationships even work. I lost my mind, got rejected, went on cringe dates, swiped right on hundreds of Tinder profiles—and somehow ended up married to Lusia.

Everything turned out great and wonderful for me, but friends around me (and for a while I myself) kept talking about how badly they wanted an interesting, lovely girl they could be happily in love with. And for some reason everyone always ran into a ton of conflicts, embarrassing stories, and mismatched people on the way to those interesting and lovely girls.

My path turned out short, but packed with lessons I had to learn and rakes I could have avoided. I deeply sympathize with anyone who is trying to figure out the same thing now. I am not sure you can get any takeaways from someone else’s experience in matters like this, but if my story helps someone, that would be wonderful.

All the names are chosen at random except for Lusia’s; any resemblance is a coincidence, and I swear I did everything possible so the stories cause as little moral damage as possible to the girls mentioned.

---

## Before the breakup

My university experience began with a breakup. At the end of the first semester I broke up with Nina, whom I had been dating for about a year.

Before that I was always the one being dumped. Those breakups went the “it’s not you, it’s me” route—and it really wasn’t mostly about me. Those breakups were very painful at first, but there was nothing you could do and things eventually went back to normal.

With Nina the relationship was so emotionally exhausting that I left by the “saving a drowning person is the work of the drowning person themselves” principle.

Back then I lived on endorphin rollercoasters. I always felt great when it was just the two of us in normal circumstances. The problem was that being with Nina in normal circumstances was impossible—she was consistently an hour or two late and cancelled dates at the last minute. Around our mutual friends she almost ignored me. Nina was physically incapable of answering messages and calmly ignored chats for days.

So to have any quality time together I first had to get lucky. Sometimes I got lucky, sometimes I didn’t. That is how I developed a classic gambling addiction pattern. When I was lucky, I got exactly the relationship I wanted at the time. When I wasn’t, I was devastated that things fell apart again—and immediately hoped that next time I would definitely get lucky.

Back then I thought we just needed to settle in, get used to it, change my attitude toward the situation. That logic ran into the fact that my relationship was tightly intertwined with the rest of my life. Planning a daytime walk for a three-hour window often turned into me waiting for an hour until Nina bailed. After that I would rejigger what to do with the remaining two hours, and our meeting got postponed for several days, when the whole thing could repeat again.

Messages, calls, reminders, alarms, subtracting time from the actual scheduled time—nothing worked on Nina. She could forget to reply, oversleep, get sick, miss the notification, or be distracted by an urgent matter. She had a perfectly valid explanation for any complaint I raised.

I have always been bad at confrontation and tried not to snap at people for things that were not their fault. So in each particular situation it felt strange to argue, but at the same time the resentment kept piling up. That accumulated resentment felt like a heavy burden and my fault—if we had just talked everything through, why was I still unhappy?

At some point I realized that every new excuse increased my hope that next time I would get lucky because that particular problem would never happen again. Unfortunately the excuses started repeating.

![](/images/loves-and-breakups-1.jpg)

I realize how incredibly dumb this sounds. It is dumb that I am writing several paragraphs about a problem that can be solved with clocks and notifications. And if they don’t solve it, then agreements that both sides stick to will. But, damn, I even turned those agreements into new slot machines, justifying occasional violations with occasional compliance.

Now I think that all those situations were about a lack of attention and commitment that I felt but could not articulate. I backed myself into a stupid corner: I felt bad for a structural reason, but ignored the structural reason and looked only at the local symptoms. I tried not to fight because I knew fights were bad, so instead of an honest “this doesn’t work for me” I built myself an emotional casino. I treated breaking up as my personal failure, so I dragged around negativity that I could have simply dropped and moved on with a healthier “it didn’t work out—whatever” attitude.

But I let things go so far that at some point I started focusing on conflicts and the bad time without Nina more than on the good time we spent together. That was about when I started thinking I needed to change something.

## Breakup

We broke up on a conditionally good note—it felt like we just were not compatible in how we did relationships.

About a week after the breakup it first occurred to me that Nina actually had agency in her actions—and if I was wrong to end up in a relationship that didn’t suit me, she was wrong in the situations we fought over. I started looking at everything that happened from the outside, unpacked each conflict separately. The analysis ended with the conclusion of “they didn’t give a damn about me.”

Given all the endorphin swings that came before, I didn’t just start thinking badly of Nina; I straight-up lost it over the whole topic. It was impossible to figure out what parts of the past I had embellished and what parts I hadn’t. That sense that you can tell reality from imagination got shaken. I couldn’t tell how real the past year of my life even was.

I was full of uncertainty and resentment. On top of that, breakups simply hurt. Everything turned into one big clump of negative emotions triggered by any association. And obviously associations pop up here and there all the time.

On the other hand, I was still fiending for that relationship and those endorphin highs. I knew that if I forgave something or got back together, like I had done before, it would end badly. But I couldn’t trust myself not to do it—sorry, that is how withdrawal and fixation work.

I kept running into Nina, and each encounter detonated that clump of emotions in my head. It felt so bad in the moment that I literally built an association for myself that meeting Nina was dangerous. And my experience told me that sometimes she might want to find me herself, and then I would have no way out.

So I was terrified of walking around campus where she could show up with friends. I was scared to be in the neighborhoods she frequented. If I saw someone in a crowd who looked like her, I could snap and fall out of reality for a while—just forgetting where I was walking, who I was with, what we were talking about.

![](/images/loves-and-breakups-2.jpg)

One of the most embarrassing stories of my life started when I noticed Nina standing outside my seminar room for half an hour, occasionally peeking through the window. I am sure she was waiting for some friend from the adjacent classroom. But for me that story ended when I threw up on the metro during rush hour the next day. Somewhere in the middle there were panic attacks, wine, and court. I might not mind telling the whole story someday, but I still do not understand how to lay out the causal chain and wrap it in words.

For a long time I flinched at the mention of Nina and had no idea what to do with it. Overall I just tried to live on, gradually finding safe locations for myself—and in Moscow that was easy to do. I thought a lot about everything, so the clump of emotions slowly dissolved.

But during that period I wrote a lot of angry posts, said nasty things to people’s faces, behind their backs, and in chats—and that is kind of sad. I could have gone through the breakup with more dignity on that front.

## On religion

After the breakup I went through withdrawal from those endorphin rollercoasters. Before the breakup I felt bad about half the time, and afterward I felt bad constantly.

My wonderful classmate whom I complained to handled it like a true fixer: “Okay, my friend Vika just moved to Moscow to study, she also recently broke up with a guy, she doesn’t know anyone in the city, I will introduce you, you two can date.”

That is how I met the sweet girl Vika. She was kind and pleasant to talk to—we definitely wouldn’t have made it long-term, but we could spend time together nicely until we got tired of each other. I am probably a little ashamed of that motivation now, but neither of us got hurt in the end, and it really helped me back then (and maybe helped her too).

About a month later I dumped Vika over text while she was away on a three-week trip. When she came back we met to talk things through. I honestly don’t remember what I told her, but when we said goodbye she told me, “It’s probably even right that you dumped me. I’m listening to you and I hear how you are trying to invent a new religion. Maybe you should just accept one that already exists?”

So yeah, I was losing my mind at the time. I do not know what else to say.

## Quarantine

Right before quarantine a girl named Nadya suddenly invited me to her birthday. There must have been a reason because we had seen each other recently in a big group. But she invited me alone, and we were never really friends. That was probably some sign of attention from her. And Nadya was very beautiful, so I decided it would be great to get closer. We started texting, and then lockdown hit. We ended up stuck in neighboring districts.

During that quarantine life felt frozen—everyone moved to Zoom and kept doing things out of inertia, but it felt like nothing was actually happening. So our relationship got preserved in that state too: we were texting, watching some show together over Zoom, occasionally bumping into each other.

Another one of my most embarrassing stories is from the lockdown era—it feels like a time capsule from back then. I left home to go on a walk with Nadya, made it to her neighborhood through the dead city, and by the time I arrived my stomach cramped. There was not a single open fast-food place with a restroom because everything was closed. Taxis required QR codes, social cards were blocked, so I couldn’t even ride home. The situation was so no-win that I made an executive decision: once I met up with Nadya we would plan a route past a forest park so I could hop over the fence (parks were closed too), take care of business in the bushes, and continue the date without intrusive thoughts. A dog barking somewhere inside greeted me when I jumped the fence, but the problem was solved.

There was a flaw in my plan: that day I was apparently severely poisoned by an undercooked omelet, and ten minutes after the forest park my number one priority again became not to crap myself. I had to pretend some urgent business came up, bail on the date, and rush home to save the situation. Thankfully the taxi driver was understanding and scanned his QR code instead of mine.

Nadya handled herself very gracefully and ignored my weird behavior that day. When the restrictions eased and people were allowed outside on specific days, we started going on walks more often. But you had to invite and pull Nadya into things, write first—she responded to everything, agreed to everything, but never made the first move.

![](/images/loves-and-breakups-3.jpg)

At some point I realized nothing was stopping me from dating Nadya—I just had to take the first step and she would agree. And right then I thought, why the hell do I need this. I pictured how many first steps I would have to keep making, and decided the whole thing was pointless. I stopped texting first and, I think, have not really heard from her since.

That was an important moment for me because before that I always said “yes” to relationships. There was not even a question. Like in that scene from Modern Family:

> -- Girls!
>
> -- But not the kind we want.
>
> -- They are all the kind we want!

And that was the first time I felt like I didn’t need it. I am very proud of my past self for saving a lot of time on a dead-end relationship.

## Social hoeing

After quarantine I went back to scrolling through Tinder and being sad that building relationships online was impossible—it is too easy to like the next card and too hard to start a meaningful conversation. Now I would only use Tinder if I limited myself to ten swipes a day and went on a date with every match, just to cap the swiping and increase the conversion from swipe to conversation. In parallel with Tinder I was hoeing socially both offline and online, running a blog and writing things.

My Tinder dates regularly served up some kind of cringe:

- I went on a Tinder date with a girl from Irkutsk who forgot to mention in her profile that she was in Moscow for only one day—so I got a two-hour lecture about Irkutsk accompanied by nature photos.
- I had Tinder guests with a girl who very suddenly invited me over late at night with the comment “my parents are gone.” When I arrived she would not stop chattering about how cool she was, how cool her ex was, how cool they were together. A few hours later, around 3 a.m., she kicked me out because, it turned out, her parents were supposed to come back early in the morning.
- I had a date where halfway through I learned the girl was literally my mom’s student and was telling me gossip about the philology department that I already knew—from the other side.

Even though I fussed over photos and bios and constantly experimented with my profile, Tinder just did not click. Meanwhile the shitposting online actually led to something!

For example, back in school I knew an insanely beautiful and sweet girl named Bella, and I probably could not make myself walk up to her for two years. We crossed paths every few months at some events, and all I could do was mumble awkwardly and imagine how next time I would definitely ask her out. That was not really like me; I usually felt comfortable in situations like that. But she was the kind of girl who made me tremble.

And then she texted me herself after some post! We started talking, set up a meeting, had a lovely walk. A few walks later I suggested we date! And she said she didn’t need that. I was upset, of course, but I got over it.

![](/images/loves-and-breakups-4.jpg)

I also met Dina through the blog, and she was a straight 10 out of 10. We vibed unbelievably well, on some impossible level. And she had a boyfriend. It was a fascinating experience because I felt that stereotypical male competition—despite the fact that I never even saw him, the very fact of his existence blocked our relationship with Dina.

We often hung out, partied, went places together—and she was the perfect person for that. Dina was an overachiever like me. She spoke as fast and indistinctly as I did. She was always signing up for some kind of activity like I was. She ran a blog like me. She taught like me.

But she also had the same cockroaches I did. We were two puzzle pieces sticking out with the exact same tabs, so you could not connect us. Our combined anxiety would have been enough for five people. And I accepted that it was better not to try building a relationship with her.

Some time later Dina broke up with her boyfriend, and we tried being FWB—friends with benefits—for a month or two. The arrangement worked like this: we hung out just like before, but sometimes went home together to drink wine and have sex, and sometimes we went our separate ways. We did not really claim a relationship—Dina could easily go on a trip and sleep with other guys, and I liked how easy it was to exit the situation. I thought it was a pretty successful experience!

And I had no trouble stepping out of that FWB situation once I met Lusia.

## Lusia

I have already said that my Tinder dates were cringe.

So when after my first date with Lusia I found myself wanting a second one—that was surprising.

We met on Bumble, which is like Tinder but slightly better. At the time I was still seeing Dina, so I hardly ever opened dating apps. I went on a date with Lusia not because I was actively looking for a relationship but because an opportunity to have a good time came up.

The dates were really good—Lusia is easy to interact with, and you can feel it when you barely know each other and go for beers at Ptitsa Sinitsa. And it was wildly flattering that a girl four years older than me was going on dates with me and having fun. A few weeks later we started dating.

Honestly it is hard to tell any spicy story about Lusia because a good story requires some conflict—and we simply did not have much of that. We got along easily, felt comfortable around each other, had great sex—the full package!

I sped up our relationship three times in a big way.

The first time was when I invited her to Voronezh with me for a week. We had been together maybe a month or a month and a half, and it was not very clear how we would survive a week together. But I planned to run orienteering, so in the worst case I would spend the whole day in the forest and we wouldn’t even see each other. We found this hilarious red apartment with a jacuzzi that we nicknamed the temple of sex, bought tickets, and went on a fantastic trip! I ran orienteering, we walked around Voronezh, cooked together, vibed and matched. That was about when I freaked out over how smoothly our shared leisure and day-to-day life worked together without any extra tuning. And around then I first thought I would not be surprised if we got married.

The second time was when, a month after Voronezh, I suggested we move in together. My lease in Konkovo was ending, and we could rent something closer to the center for the two of us. We found a wonderful apartment on Malaya Andronyevskaya and lived there for about seven months.

That was a trickier experience because we ran into some friction points and different conflict situations—we had not known each other that long, and our cohabitation in Voronezh had only lasted a week.

For example, the apartment that Lusia clung to tooth and nail had one and a half rooms instead of the two we wanted, and the only separate room was entirely occupied by a Romanian wardrobe. The kitchen was tiny, you could not turn around, and there wasn’t even a bed for us—just a couch. Lusia’s artistic soul cared more about the parquet, the wooden table, and the romantic first floor. So in the first days of living together I was in shock that a renovation awaited me in a rental. After a while we disassembled and carried out the Romanian wardrobe, bought and assembled an IKEA bed, and the apartment ended up incredibly warm and beloved.

That was when some insecurities started showing up for me—we were learning how to fight and make up, to read our behavior when one of us felt bad, and how best to act in those situations.

The third time I sped things up was when we moved after the war started. That massively increased my responsibility for her—I was leaving because I had to, and she was leaving because I was.

After the move everything was just hard—Lusia’s dad died, she was depressed, I was trying not to get expelled and to find a job that would support us. We were both struggling with our own problems and often didn’t have the strength to support each other. It was a tough period that I am, in some sense, still recovering from. Around that time, in the summer of 2022, we got married—partly to make paperwork easier in the future, and partly to feel a little calmer in the moment knowing you are not alone. We had a small wedding with two witnesses, a party that was mostly an excuse for all our Moscow friends to make it to Tbilisi, and we also gathered our families in Batumi.

You can tell the circumstances were not exactly wedding-friendly—and that is exactly how it was. But when the world is on fire, the people who are always by your side (emotionally and legally) are incredibly valuable. The two of us in emigration were de facto ready for that commitment, so it would have been strange to postpone it.

![](/images/loves-and-breakups-5.jpg)

Over the time with Lusia I figured out a ton of crap about myself. I started noticing more often that some of my behavior was problematic and systemic—and in some cases I even managed to fix it! Even though we match really well, we still sometimes run into tricky situations that require us to talk things through in a safe atmosphere and find a solution that works for both of us. That sounds absurdly goofy, exhausting, and like a psychologist picked the words. Honestly, it feels the same. To fix something we talk for a long, draining time until we arrive at something. Along the way we remind ourselves that reaching some kind of resolution is more important than being right in the argument, and that we love our partner very much, and most likely that love is bigger than the annoyance from the fight. And basically this dumb-as-a-brick method is still working, surprisingly enough.

My current experience living with Lusia shows that having a family is awesome—sometimes I choose more boring activities in the moment, but we drastically improve our overall quality of life every day with shared meals, watching shows together, dumb jokes. On the one hand I am becoming more boring and starting to prefer routine, and on the other hand it is precisely thanks to Lusia that I value that routine so highly. And to be honest, after these four years I do not have any energy left for new thrills—let me relax with my wife in peace!

## Instead of an ending

I started university with a breakup and I am finishing it as part of the Amelichev-Svinarenko family (or, as we call it, the Caramelichev-Confectenkov clan).

When the war began and we were packing to leave, I texted Nina that it was stupid of me to hold a grudge against her two years later when everything around us was going to hell. She recently came to Tbilisi—she does not look like the demonized version from my head, just a normal girl. We had beers and talked about life.

Honestly I cannot tell what mattered more for my personal growth—finding closure around the breakup or the relationship that happened with Lusia. I am proud of myself for surviving the terrifying phase of the breakup and later managing to let go of the resentment. In the case of my relationship with Lusia everything developed so naturally, easily, and playfully when we met that my main growth point was simply being ready for it when I met her: being honest with her and with myself, not getting into relationships that I knew from the outset did not fit me, taking everything lightly enough. And, let’s be frank, hoeing socially to increase my chances of meeting someone.

So yeah, that is where things stand. My love life right now is not unfolding in any particularly cinematic way. Lately Lusia has been sorting out her career questions, and I am standing behind her, supporting her. We are moving to Paris soon—who knows what will happen there?
